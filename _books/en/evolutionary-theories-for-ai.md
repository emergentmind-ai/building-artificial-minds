## The book in one sentence

Evolution is the only process known to us that has demonstrably produced intelligence—yet AI research has so far largely pursued different paths.


## Abstract

Artificial intelligence is one of the most ambitious technological endeavors of our time. Ever larger models are trained on ever more data, while increasingly powerful computers process ever more complex patterns. The results are impressive. Yet the question remains whether more data and ever larger models are truly the right path.

This book takes a different perspective. The only form of intelligence whose emergence we can actually explain is biological intelligence, and the only process we know with certainty to have produced intelligence is evolution.

Thinking, learning, and understanding did not emerge through optimization or planning, but through a blind, unguided process of variation, selection, and adaptation. Evolution did not merely produce life — it produced the only intelligent systems we know.

The book explores what artificial intelligence can learn from this fact.

To that end, it begins with the fundamentals. It explains what evolution actually is and why it is often misunderstood. Evolution is not optimization, nor does it pursue a goal. Its strength lies not in efficiently solving known problems, but in producing new solutions that no one could have foreseen.

From there, the book traces the path from simple evolutionary mechanisms to increasingly complex forms of adaptation. It shows how genes store information, how biological systems grow through modularity, how learning emerges from evolution, and how, ultimately, thinking, understanding, and intelligence can emerge.

Throughout, this perspective is continually applied to modern AI. Evolutionary algorithms, artificial life, open-ended simulations, and adaptive agents are considered not merely as technical tools, but as potential alternatives to a purely design-oriented approach to artificial intelligence. While many current AI systems are focused on optimization within predefined objectives, evolution continuously generates novelty, adaptability, and open-ended development.

The central thesis of the book is that AI's greatest challenges — creativity, generalization, robustness, and genuine understanding — may not be solvable through ever larger models and ever more data. Evolution might not just be a tool for AI, but its missing foundation.

If we want to build intelligent systems, we should pay more attention to the only known process that has already produced them: evolution.
